# Part 1 · Project for an Aeronautical Machine
### Project for an Aeronautical Machine
**N. I. Kibal'chich**  
*March 23, 1881*

---

I am writing this project in prison, a few days before my death. I believe in the feasibility of my idea, and this belief sustains me in my terrible predicament. If my idea, after being evaluated by scientific experts, is recognized as feasible, I will be happy with the thought that I have rendered a great service to my country and mankind. I can then calmly meet death, knowing that my idea will not perish with me, but will remain among humanity for whom I am ready to sacrifice my life. That is why I pray those scientists who will examine my project to treat it with the greatest seriousness and conscientiousness, and to give me their answer as soon as possible.

First of all, I must note that while at liberty, I had no time to elaborate my project in detail and to prove its feasibility by mathematical calculations. At present, I naturally have no opportunity to obtain the materials necessary for such work. This task—namely, the substantiation of my project by mathematical calculations—must therefore be performed by those experts into whose hands my project will fall.

Furthermore, I am not acquainted with the mass of similar projects that have appeared recently; that is to say, more precisely, I know the ideas underlying these projects, but I do not know in what specific forms inventors intend to realize them. As far as I know, however, my idea has not yet been proposed by anyone.

In considering an aeronautical machine, I first addressed the question: what power should be used to propel this machine? From an *a priori* consideration, one can say that steam power is completely unsuitable here. I cannot recall precisely what fraction of the heat energy imparted to steam is utilized as mechanical work, but I do know that this fraction is quite small. Furthermore, a steam engine is cumbersome and requires a large quantity of coal for its operation. Therefore, I believe that no matter what devices—such as wings, propellers, etc.—are attached to a steam engine, it will be incapable of lifting itself into the air.

In electric motors, the fraction of input energy transformed into useful work is far greater, but to drive large electric motors, one still requires a steam engine. Suppose we could build steam engines and generators on the ground and transmit electric power to the flying machine through wires similar to telegraph wires, with the machine sliding along the wires via special metal contact shoes to obtain power for wings or similar mechanisms. I shall not attempt to prove whether building such an apparatus is possible, but even if it were, it would be inconvenient, expensive, and would offer no significant advantages over railway transport.

Many inventors base the locomotion of aeronautical apparatuses on human muscular power, as did, for example, Dr. Arendt. In conceiving their conceptual machines, they take birds as models, believing it possible to construct an apparatus capable of lifting itself and flying through the air propelled solely by the flyer's own strength. In my opinion, even if an apparatus of this type could be built, it could serve at best as a toy, without possessing any serious practical value.

What power, then, is applicable to aeronautics? In my view, this power is found in slowly burning explosive substances.

Indeed, during the combustion of explosive substances, a large volume of gas is generated sooner or later, and at the moment of its generation, this gas possesses immense energy. I cannot recall precisely how many kilogram-meters of work can be performed by one pound of gunpowder upon ignition, but if I remember correctly, one pound of gunpowder exploded underground can throw up 40 poods (an old Russian unit; 1 pood = 36 pounds [approx. 16.38 kg]) of earth. In short, no other substance in nature can release such enormous energy in so short a time as an explosive substance.

How, then, can the energy of gases generated by the ignition of explosives be utilized to perform prolonged work? This is possible under only one condition: namely, that the enormous energy produced by the combustion of explosives must not be released all at once, but over a more or less prolonged period.

If we take a pound of granulated gunpowder, it will certainly flare up in an instant upon ignition; but if we compress it under high pressure into a cylindrical shape and then ignite one end of the cylinder, we will find that the flame does not engulf the entire cylinder immediately, but propagates rather slowly from one end to the other at a definite speed. Numerous experiments have established that the rate of combustion propagation in compressed powder is 4 lines per second (four-tenths of an inch per second—see Note 1 at the end of the text).

This property of compressed powder is essentially the operating principle of the military rocket. The essence of this principle is as follows: into a tin cylinder, closed at one end and open at the other, a cylinder of compressed powder is tightly packed, having a hole along its axis in the form of a continuous channel; combustion of the compressed powder begins on the inner surface of this channel and spreads over a definite period of time toward the outer surface of the powder; the gases formed by the combustion exert pressure in all directions, but the lateral pressures mutually balance each other, whereas the pressure exerted against the closed bottom of the tin casing containing the powder, not being balanced by an opposing pressure (since the gases have a free exit in that direction), propels the rocket forward in the direction set on the launching ramp prior to ignition. The trajectory of the rocket is a parabola similar to that of a bullet fired from a firearm.

Now let us imagine that we have a cylinder of tinplate of known dimensions, hermetically sealed on all sides, with an opening of specific size only in its bottom. Let us place along the axis a cylindrical block of compressed powder and ignite it from one of its bases.[^1] Upon burning, gases will be formed that exert pressure on the entire inner surface of the metal cylinder; the pressures on the sides mutually cancel, but the pressure exerted by the gases on the sealed top of the cylinder cannot be balanced by opposing pressure, because on the opposite side the gases can escape freely through the opening in the bottom. If this cylinder is placed with its sealed top upward, then at a certain gas pressure—the magnitude of which depends on the internal volume of the cylinder on the one hand, and on the thickness of the compressed powder cylinder on the other—the cylinder must rise.

[^1]: Author's note: I do not know precisely whether, in order to maintain conditions of slow and uniform combustion, it is necessary to encase the compressed powder in a tightly fitting jacket. But even if it must be encased, this would in no way hinder the utilization of compressed powder in the construction of the apparatus.

I have no data at hand that would allow me to determine, even approximately, what quantity of compressed powder must be burned per unit time so that, in a cylinder of known dimensions and known tare weight, the gases produced by combustion would exert sufficient pressure on the bottom to counter the force of gravity acting on the cylinder. But I believe this problem is completely solvable in practice: that is, for a given cylinder size and weight, by using compressed powder cylinders of known thickness, it is entirely possible to achieve a gas pressure on the top that balances the weight of the cylinder. Rockets themselves provide conclusive proof of this. Rockets currently manufactured can lift explosive projectiles weighing up to 5 poods (180 pounds). True, the example of rockets is not entirely applicable here, because rockets possess extremely high velocities, which would be unthinkable in an aeronautical machine; but the high speed of rockets results from the enormous quantity of compressed powder loaded into them and its extensive burning surface area. If a much slower rate of ascent is desired, the quantity of powder burned per unit time must be correspondingly reduced.

We thus arrive at a schematic description of my apparatus (Figure 1).

In cylinder A, which has an opening C in its bottom, a powder candle K (as I designate small compressed powder cylinders) is mounted along the axis near the top. Cylinder A is secured by means of upright struts N to the center of a platform P, upon which the aeronaut must stand. To ignite the powder candles and replace them with new ones as they burn out (during which combustion must not be interrupted, of course), a novel automatic mechanism should be devised. For reloading immediately upon burnout, the most suitable automatic device would be one driven by clockwork, owing to the uniform and predictable burning process of the powder candles.

I shall not dwell on these auxiliary mechanisms, however, since all such problems can be easily resolved by modern technology.

Now let us imagine that powder candle K has been ignited. After an extremely brief interval, cylinder A is filled with incandescent gases, a portion of whose pressure acts against the top of the cylinder; if this pressure exceeds the combined weight of the cylinder, platform, and aeronaut, the apparatus must rise.

We note parenthetically that the ascent of this apparatus is facilitated not only by the pressure of the powder gases; the specific gravity of the hot gases filling cylinder A is less than that of the air displaced by them. Therefore, according to the laws of aerostatics, the apparatus must be lightened by an amount equal to the difference between the weight of the air filling cylinder A and the weight of the powder gases contained therein (see Note 2). Thus, we once again encounter conditions favorable to the ascent of an aerostat; by virtue of gas pressure, the apparatus can rise very high, provided that the gas pressure acting against the top exceeds the weight of the apparatus during ascent. If it is desired to remain hovering motionless at a specific altitude, powder candles of smaller thickness must be employed, so that the generated gas pressure exactly equals the weight of the apparatus.

In this manner, the aeronautical apparatus can be brought into a relationship with the air medium analogous to that of a steamship floating motionless upon the surface of water. How, then, are we to propel our apparatus in a desired direction? Two methods are proposed for this purpose.

We can employ a second, similar cylinder, oriented horizontally, with the nozzle opening in its base facing laterally rather than downward. If a similar arrangement with powder candles is installed in this cylinder and ignited, the gases impinging against the bottom of the cylinder will propel the apparatus in the direction toward which its base is oriented. In order that the horizontal cylinder may be aimed in any direction, it must be capable of rotating in the horizontal plane. A compass may be used to determine the course, just as in maritime navigation.

In my opinion, however, we can manage with only a single cylinder if we construct it so that it can be tilted in the vertical plane and, at the same time, rotated in a conical fashion. Both the suspension of the apparatus in the air and its translation in the horizontal direction can be accomplished through the inclination of the cylinder.

![Inclined Thrust Vector Decomposition (Figure 2)](/images/rocket-pioneers/kibalchich-fig2.png)
*Figure 2*

Let us assume that the force produced by the gas pressure on the bottom of the cylinder is represented graphically as $P$; we decompose this force into components $Q$ and $R$. If component $Q$ equals the weight of the apparatus, the apparatus will fly in the horizontal plane propelled by force $R$. Consequently, the cylinder must be inclined to such an extent that flight can proceed in the horizontal plane. To steer it in a predetermined direction, its axis must be aimed along that direction by revolving the cylinder conically.

Nevertheless, it seems to me that by employing two cylinders we can obtain an apparatus with superior maneuverability and stability. Indeed, with two cylinders, vibrations of the apparatus are less likely to deflect it from its designated path, whereas a single cylinder is more vulnerable to disturbances. Furthermore, with a single cylinder it is more difficult to achieve the velocities attainable with two cylinders.

Regarding the stability of the apparatus, I consider it sufficient, since the cylinder is located above the heavier portions of the apparatus, so that the center of gravity of at least one cylinder (for example, the upper one) lies on the same vertical line as the center of gravity of the apparatus. Nevertheless, certain motion regulators (such as wings, etc.) could be designed to ensure stability.

To cause the apparatus to descend back to the ground, powder candles of progressively smaller diameter must be installed, whereupon the apparatus will descend gently.

In conclusion, I wish to point out that compressed powder is by no means the only medium suitable for this purpose. Many slow-burning explosives exist that contain, like gunpowder, potassium nitrate, sulfur, and charcoal, but in different proportions or admixed with other substances. Perhaps among these compositions some will prove more convenient than compressed powder.

Only through experiment can it be determined whether my idea is truly valid. Likewise, only through experiment will it be possible to establish the necessary quantitative relationships among cylinder dimensions, powder candle thickness, and the gross weight of the lifted apparatus. Preliminary experiments could easily be conducted using quite small cylinders, even indoors.

**March 23, 1881**

## Commentary
*(From page 14 of the original / Russian edition p. 638)*

This article was written by N. I. Kibal'chich in prison a few days before his execution. In the records of the interrogation of the "March 1 Revolutionaries," it is noted that Kibal'chich's defense attorney, V. N. Gerard, upon his first interview with his client, was astonished to find him "completely absorbed in something entirely unrelated to the trial. He was immersed in the study of a certain flying machine; he was passionately eager to have the opportunity to write down the mathematical derivations connected with this invention. He wrote them down and submitted them to the authorities" (cited from Kibal'chich Literature 4).

However, Kibal'chich's wish to have his project evaluated by scientific experts was not fulfilled. On the official dossier accompanying the project, the following endorsement was made: "To be attached to the file of the March 1 case. Submitting it to scientists for consideration at this time is hardly appropriate, and could only give rise to improper interpretations" (cited from Kibal'chich Literature 5, p. 235).

Receiving no reply, Kibal'chich addressed a second petition to the Minister of Internal Affairs, requesting permission to meet with scientists in person, or at least to obtain their written review of his invention (cited from Kibal'chich Literature 5, p. 280a), but this request was likewise ignored.

Following Kibal'chich's execution, his project was filed away in the archives of the Police Department. While reports of the project appeared in the press, they were distorted beyond recognition.

Only after the February Revolution of 1917 was the project retrieved from the archives and published with notes by N. A. Rynin in the journal *Byloye* (cited from Kibal'chich Literature 1). In the present collection, the text is printed from the original holographic manuscript, preserved in the Central State Archive of the October Revolution in Moscow (cited from Kibal'chich Literature 4, pp. 1–5; unsigned holograph).

- **Note 1 (Original p. 10)**: 1 line (Russian *liniya*) $= 2.54\ \text{mm}$ ($0.1\ \text{inch}$).
- **Note 2 (Original p. 12)**: The buoyancy of the hot combustion gases mentioned here has no practical significance in engineering.
