## Manuscript History and Four Evolutionary Versions

According to Yuri Vasilievich Kondratyuk's own written statements, his deep academic interest in the scientific problems of interplanetary flight began in 1916 (see the Author's Preface to his 1929 monograph *The Conquest of Interplanetary Space*, also in this anthology, p. 57).

Based on the original archival manuscripts preserved in the Central State Archive of the October Revolution in Moscow, the Editorial Board has identified four primary evolutionary stages of Kondratyuk's astronautical theory:

1. **First Draft Manuscript (1916–Early 1917)**:  
   In this original working draft, the author independently derived the differential equation of rocket motion for the first time, exploring the overall architecture of interplanetary spacecraft, the dynamical conditions of flight within the Solar System, the establishment of orbital staging bases, the influence of planetary atmospheres upon vehicle reentry, and the utilization of solar radiant energy in space. This was a working draft not intended for formal publication.

2. **Second Manuscript (1918–1919)—Translated in this Part as *To Whomsoever Will Read in Order to Build***:  
   Building upon the first draft, Kondratyuk greatly expanded and deepened his work during his secluded stay in Poltava. Beyond extensive stylistic polishing, he incorporated several pioneering, independent chapters, including "A Revolutionary Scheme to Elevate Human Physiological Overload Tolerances," "Gravitational Assists from the Motion of Celestial Bodies," and "Space Thin-Film Solar Concentrators." The manuscript is penned in neat, clear script and accompanied by numerous detailed engineering schematic drawings, constituting the complete text presented in this Part.

3. **Third Manuscript (1920–1925)**:  
   During this interval, the author incorporated more rigorous analytical mechanics, conducting quantitative mathematical investigations into powered-phase trajectory ascent, atmospheric aerodynamic drag losses, and propellant thermochemistry. In 1925, this manuscript was submitted to the Main Administration of Scientific Institutions (Glavnauka) in Moscow for review, where it received an exceptionally favorable evaluation from the eminent aerodynamicist Professor V. P. Vetchinkin.

4. **Fourth Version (Published Definitive 1929 Edition and 1938 Academy Archival Manuscript)**:  
   Drawing upon the third draft as its foundation, the author self-published his classic landmark treatise *The Conquest of Interplanetary Space* in Novosibirsk, Siberia, in 1929 (Part III of this anthology). In 1938, Kondratyuk formally transmitted his original first and second manuscripts in their entirety to the Commission on the History of Natural Science and Technology of the Academy of Sciences of the USSR for permanent preservation.

## Complete Text of the 28 Technical Notes by the Editorial Board

### Note 1 (p. 18): The Magnitude of Escape Velocity
The value of approximately $11\ \text{km/s}$ given here by Kondratyuk refers to the theoretical second cosmic velocity (escape velocity $V_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2gR} \approx 11.18\ \text{km/s}$) required to escape Earth's gravitational field, under the idealized assumption neglecting rotational velocity contributions from the Earth's surface.

### Note 2 (p. 21): Specification of the Absolute CGS System
The absolute physical unit system employed by the author throughout the manuscript is the Gaussian CGS system (centimeters-grams-seconds). The dimension of $rj$ is $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}^2$ (i.e., specific potential energy per unit mass, $\text{ergs/g}$).

### Note 3 (p. 21): Independent Discovery of the Tsiolkovsky Formula
The formula $V = \sqrt{2p} \ln(M/m)$ is mathematically identical to the famous ideal rocket velocity equation $V = c \ln(M/m)$ published by K. E. Tsiolkovsky in 1903 (where effective exhaust velocity $c = \sqrt{2p}$). Archival evidence irrefutably confirms that when Kondratyuk composed this manuscript while living in seclusion in Poltava in 1918, he was entirely unaware of any of Tsiolkovsky's previous publications; it represents an entirely independent, parallel scientific rediscovery.

### Note 4 (p. 24): Gravity Turn and Tangential Ascent
The author's "second method of flight"—imparting thrust acceleration tangentially, perpendicular to the local gravitational radius vector—is conceptually identical to the modern **gravity turn trajectory** into orbit. Along this profile, centrifugal acceleration rapidly balances gravitational pull, minimizing gravity-drag velocity losses to the theoretical minimum.

### Note 5 (p. 25): Hydrostatic Anti-g Immersion Bath
Kondratyuk was the first in world scientific history to identify the physical feasibility of immersing the human body in an iso-dense fluid bath to withstand extreme acceleration. Modern aerospace medical research (such as centrifuge trials with animals and human subjects) has thoroughly corroborated that under complete fluid immersion, because hydrostatic pressures across internal thoracic-abdominal organs and the outer skin remain in hydrostatic equilibrium at every point, mammals can endure transient overloads exceeding $30\ g$ without mechanical rupture of internal viscera.

### Note 7 (p. 28): Optimum Multistage Mass Distribution
The author's concept that empty structural tank mass represents a constant fraction of the vehicle's total mass at each staging event corresponds rigorously in modern multistage rocket optimization theory to the **equal structural coefficient criterion and optimal characteristic velocity staging theorem**.

### Note 8 (p. 28): Segregated Storage of Cryogenic Propellants
Liquid oxygen (boiling point $-183^\circ\text{C}$) and liquid hydrogen (boiling point $-253^\circ\text{C}$) exhibit a thermal gradient at standard pressure, and direct contact poses extreme catastrophic detonation hazards. The author's concept of strictly isolating individual stage tanks into separate upper and lower compartments fully conforms to modern cryogenic rocket design standards.

### Note 9 (p. 28): Elastic Stress Analysis of Thin-Walled Pressure Vessels
In classical thin-shell elasticity theory, the membrane hoop tensile stress in a spherical shell under uniform internal pressure $P$ is $\sigma = \frac{Pr}{2\delta}$, and in a cylindrical shell is $\sigma = \frac{Pr}{\delta}$. The author here correctly notes the structural mass-to-volume containment ratios of these two standard geometries.

### Note 10 (p. 31): Principle of Gyroscopic Inertial Orientation
The author's design of a three-degree-of-freedom gimbaled gyroscope constrained within rigid frames establishes a spatial inertial reference via conservation of angular momentum of a high-speed rotor, representing one of the earliest engineering proposals for an autonomous inertial measurement unit (IMU) and attitude reference platform for spaceflight.

### Note 11 (p. 32): Four-Rotor Dynamic De-Spin System
A single spinning gyroscope rotor generates reactive gyroscopic precessional torques when tilted. The author cancelled these parasitic reaction couples by designing two counter-rotating, symmetrically arranged pairs of opposed rotors, ensuring absolute purity and stability for the vehicle's inertial reference frame.

### Note 12 (p. 33): Double-Door Hermetic Waste-Disposal Airlock
The author's design of a dual-door series airlock mechanism, which alternately cycles an intermediate chamber to discharge waste, prevents the catastrophic decompression of oxygenated cabin air into the vacuum of space, establishing the standard architecture for modern manned spacecraft airlocks.

### Note 13 (p. 33): Cryogenic Slush and Solidified Propellants
The author proposed storing hydrogen and oxygen in solid or subcooled slurry form—known in modern astronautics as **slush propellants**. Slush hydrogen exhibits higher physical density and lower saturated vapor pressure than standard liquid hydrogen, significantly reducing required tank volume and ullage pressurization demands.

### Note 14 (p. 34): Ground-Test Criteria for Injector Mixing
The author's insistence that the premixed, diffusion, and partially premixed injector configurations must be settled through rigorous ground-based hot-fire test stands demonstrates outstanding experimental and engineering discipline.

### Note 15 (p. 34): Pioneering Status of the Checkerboard Multielement Injector
Kondratyuk's concept of an alternating checkerboard pattern of square micro-orifices across the injector faceplate represents a brilliant milestone in rocket propulsion history. Modern high-performance liquid rocket engines (such as the Space Shuttle Main Engine SSME, the Saturn V F-1, and the RL-10) employ coaxial multi-element injector faceplates based upon identical principles of microscopic fluid interlace atomization and turbulent mixing.

### Note 16 (p. 36): Parabolic Supersonic Laval Nozzles
The flared parabolic nozzle contour independently derived by the author strictly conforms to the principles of supersonic adiabatic gas expansion in one-dimensional compressible fluid dynamics.

### Note 17 (p. 37): Panoramic Periscopic Celestial Compass
By utilizing an optical prism periscope, the cosmonaut can survey the celestial sphere from within an unpenetrated, high-strength pressurized metal sphere, eliminating the structural stress concentrations and hermetic sealing vulnerabilities inherent in large cabin viewing windows.

### Note 18 (p. 38): The "Eureka!" Note in the Margin
In the margin of this page of the original manuscript, Kondratyuk penned in bold Cyrillic capitals: "Эврика!" ("Eureka!"). The Editorial Board highlighted this moment as: *"One of the greatest and most radiant flashes of insight in the history of world astronautics—shattering in a single stroke the crushing mass penalty of having to rely entirely upon rocket retro-propellant for return to Earth."*

### Note 19 (p. 38): Exponential Reduction in Liftoff Mass via Aerodynamic Braking
When return flight no longer demands carrying propellant for a retro-burn velocity impulse $\Delta V_{\text{return}}$, the exponent in the launch mass equation drops from $2\sqrt{rj/p}$ to $\sqrt{rj/p}$. For a hydrogen-oxygen propellant system, this slashes the theoretical mass ratio from $55$ down to $7.4$!

### Note 20 (p. 39): Mass Conservation Across Deep-Space Round Trips
The author points out that because propellant is no longer wasted on powered atmospheric deceleration upon return, the massive propellant fractions formerly reserved for landing can be reallocated to deep-space velocity increments for round-trip voyages to Mars, Venus, or Jupiter.

### Note 21 (p. 39): Hypersonic Oblique Shocks and the Sine Law of Attack Angle
Modern aerothermodynamic analysis verifies that in hypervelocity continuum flow at high Mach numbers, surface stagnation heat flux and convective heat transfer scale approximately with $(V \sin \alpha)^3$. The author's qualitative insight that smaller angles of attack $\alpha$ produce milder heating rests on firm physical foundations.

### Note 22 (p. 40): High-Fineness Lifting Bodies and Winged Glider Geometry
The winged gliding reentry airframe sketched by the author exhibits an astonishing resemblance to the lifting-body and delta-wing configurations developed in the 1970s for the American Space Shuttle and the Soviet Buran orbiter.

### Note 23 (p. 41): Overcoming the Thermal Barrier at Second Cosmic Velocity
The author prophetically analyzed the severity of the thermal barrier encountered during atmospheric reentry at $11.2\ \text{km/s}$ (and higher), proposing a multifaceted thermal protection strategy: quartz/ceramic refractory composites, nose ablative shielding, and long-range skip-gliding in the rarefied upper atmosphere to extend the radiative heat dissipation window.

### Note 24 (p. 41): Rigorous Derivation of Multi-Layer Insulation (MLI)
Applying Kirchhoff's radiation laws, the author derived the quantitative relation demonstrating that interposing multiple reflective metal foils within a vacuum gap attenuates radiant heat transfer. Known today as Multi-Layer Insulation (MLI), this remains the universal thermal protection standard for deep-space probes and cryogenic propellant tanks.

### Note 25 (p. 48): Strategic Vision of Orbital Staging Bases
As early as 1919, the author systematically formulated the modern two-phase mission architecture: "Launch from Earth → Rendezvous and refueling at a low-Earth-orbit space station → Departure for interplanetary exploration."

### Note 27 (p. 49): Trajectory Planning for Gravity Acceleration and Deceleration
Flying behind a celestial body's trailing hemisphere drags the spacecraft in the direction of orbital motion, imparting a net kinetic energy boost (accelerating slingshot); flying ahead of the leading hemisphere retards the vehicle's motion, achieving unpowered gravitational braking for orbital capture.
