## Fundamental Concepts and Physical Notation

In order to examine the mechanics of interplanetary flight with the utmost rigor, we must first establish a unified system of physical definitions and mathematical symbols adhered to throughout this monograph:

### 1. Vehicle and Trajectory
* **Rocket**: A flight device moving in space without reliance on any external supporting medium (such as atmospheric support or ground reaction force), propelled solely by the reactive thrust generated by the backward high-speed ejection of matter particles carried within itself;
* **Active trajectory**: The interval of the flight trajectory during which the rocket engine is operating, continuously expending vehicle mass and producing thrust acceleration;
* **Passive trajectory**: The orbital interval after engine shutdown during which the rocket moves in unpowered, purely inertial free fall within celestial gravitational fields;
* $M$: The instantaneous total actual mass of the rocket at any given moment along its trajectory;
* $M_0$: The **initial total liftoff mass** of the rocket at the beginning of the mission;
* $M_f$: The **final residual empty mass** (payload plus structural empty deadweight) of the rocket when it reaches the end of its powered run and propellants are exhausted;
* $M_{io}$: The initial mass of the rocket at the starting instant of a specific trajectory segment (designated as the $i$-th segment);
* $M_{if}$: The final mass of the rocket at the terminating instant of that $i$-th trajectory segment.

## Microscopic Statistical Definition of Exhaust Velocity

The term **"exhaust"** refers to the macroscopic ensemble of all microscopic material particles expelled violently rearward from the interior of the rocket; the sum of the reaction thrust forces exerted by these microscopic particles upon the inner nozzle walls during expulsion imparts forward acceleration to the rocket.

Let $u$ denote the **effective exhaust velocity**, namely the relative ejection velocity of the expelled particles with respect to the rocket frame of reference at the instant they exit the nozzle discharge plane and begin moving independently of the rocket (the microscopic influence of celestial gravity during the infinitesimal particle separation time being neglected for the present).

Over any given infinitesimal time interval, we assume the effective exhaust velocity $u$ to remain constant. If individual microscopic particles leaving the nozzle possess unequal, divergent velocities due to flow nonuniformity, we define a **statistically averaged equivalent exhaust velocity $u$** such that substituting this single mean velocity for all actual divergent heterogeneous particle velocities leaves the total reactive impulse exerted on the rocket strictly unchanged.

Within an infinitesimal time element, the relative center-of-mass velocity of the expelled particle group is rigorously expressed as:
$$u = \frac{\sum (\mu_k \cdot u_k)}{\sum \mu_k}$$

where $\mu_k$ and $u_k$ represent the individual mass and individual scalar ejection velocity of each microscopic particle, respectively.

From the above definition, a crucial thermodynamic theorem follows immediately from the law of conservation of energy:  
For a given quantity of total macroscopic kinetic energy released (i.e., available work energy produced by propellant combustion, $\frac{1}{2}\sum \mu_k u_k^2$), **the average effective exhaust velocity $u$ attains its mathematical absolute maximum if and only if all microscopic particles expelled rearward possess strictly uniform and identical discharge velocities!**

Any velocity dispersion or microscopic nonuniformity inevitably degrades a portion of the available energy into useless thermal internal energy or lateral kinetic energy, thereby reducing the engine's practical thrust efficiency.

## Intrinsic Acceleration, Propellant Consumption, and Load Rating

We define $j_0$ as the **intrinsic rocket acceleration**, representing the theoretical pure thrust acceleration imparted to the rocket exclusively by the engine exhaust thrust, completely excluding external gravity or aerodynamic resistance.

By the conservation of momentum for an infinitesimal mass element:
$$j_0 = - \frac{u}{M} \cdot \frac{dM}{dt}$$

where $-\frac{dM}{dt}$ is the mass flow rate ($\text{kg/s}$) of propellant consumed and ejected per second by the engine.

We define $n$ as the **flight load rating** (mass ratio) for the entire mission:
$$n = \frac{M_0}{M_f}$$

that is, the ratio of initial liftoff mass to final empty mass:
$$M_0 = n \cdot M_f$$

Similarly, we define $n_i$ as the **segmental load rating** of the rocket in a specific active powered flight segment $i$:
$$n_i = \frac{M_{io}}{M_{if}}$$

$$M_{io} = n_i \cdot M_{if}$$

The propellant mass consumed by the rocket during that segment is:
$$\Delta M_i = M_{io} - M_{if} = M_{if} (n_i - 1)$$

If the complex mission trajectory consists of several consecutive powered burn segments (denoted successively as $a, b, c, \dots, i, \dots, z$) alternating with unpowered passive coasting phases, the total load rating for the entire mission is strictly equal to the **cumulative continuous product** of the individual segment load ratings:
$$n = n_a \cdot n_b \cdot n_c \cdots n_i \cdots n_z$$

## Definition of Characteristic Velocity (Ideal Velocity)

We define $W$ as the **characteristic velocity** (ideal velocity) of the rocket, which equals the pure mathematical time integral of the intrinsic thrust acceleration over the total operating duration $t_f$:
$$W = \int_{0}^{t_f} j_0 \, dt$$

In other words, **the characteristic velocity $W$ represents the theoretical maximum net velocity increment that the rocket would achieve by firing continuously along a fixed straight line in an ideal cosmic vacuum, entirely free from external gravitational fields and atmospheric drag!**

In the notation $j_0$, we consider only the absolute scalar magnitude of the acceleration vector, setting aside for the moment its spatial orientation.

Likewise, we define $W_i$ as the **segmental characteristic velocity** accumulated by the rocket during a specific powered flight segment $i$ (with the engine firing from ignition time $t_1$ to shutdown time $t_2$):
$$W_i = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} j_0 \, dt$$

The characteristic velocity $W$ constitutes the fundamental physical metric for evaluating a rocket's energy capacity and orbital maneuver capability.
