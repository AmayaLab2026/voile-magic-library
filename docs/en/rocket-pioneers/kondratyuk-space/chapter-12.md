# Chapter 12 · General Perspectives and Engineering-Economic Assessment
### Section XII. General Perspectives
**By Yuriy Vasil'yevich Kondratyuk (Yu. V. Kondratyuk)**  
*(From NASA TT F-9285, pp. 123–128 / Original work, pp. 84–90)*

---

The most fundamental parameter determining the future development of interplanetary flight (at least during its initial exploratory stages) is the ratio of initial launch mass to final payload, namely the **flight load rating $n$ ($n = M_0 / M_f$)**. This numerical factor directly dictates the economic viability of the entire astronautical undertaking; from the standpoint of theoretical mechanics, flight itself presents no insurmountable obstacles.

The required propellant load (fuel and oxidizer), and consequently the approximate launch cost (under conditions where proportional passive deadweight is fully utilized as combustible reaction mass, as discussed on page 72), is **strictly proportional to the quantity $(n - 1)$**.

---

## I. Propellant Energetics and Baseline Theoretical Exhaust Velocity

At our present stage of development, we cannot yet determine with precision how lightweight individual structural assemblies can be fabricated, nor what the upper ceiling of engine energy conversion efficiency will be, nor which chemical propellant formulations and mixture ratios will prove superior.

In our preliminary estimates, based upon the mechanical work extracted from high-temperature combustion gases in existing internal combustion engines and preliminary thermodynamic derivations, we assume an average overall engine thermal conversion efficiency across the flight profile of:

$$\eta_{\text{eng}} \approx 0.8$$

The average calorific value of the propellants is taken as:

$$q_{\text{cal}} = 3.3\ \text{kcal/g} = 13.8\ \text{MJ/kg}$$

From these baseline data, the theoretical effective exhaust velocity is calculated as:

$$u = 4700\ \text{m/s}$$

*(Editor's Note: See Appendix Note 15)*. Given that test-stand data from static rocket firings are not yet available, we adopt this assumed exhaust velocity as a uniform baseline across all subsequent engineering estimates, assuming that the error in the resulting calculated mass ratio $n$ will not exceed a factor of $n^{1/10}$ in either direction.

Because aerodynamic drag losses $L_d$ during atmospheric ascent are relatively minor (as established in Chapter 8), we take the characteristic velocity required to escape Earth's gravitational field as:

$$W_e = 12,000\ \text{m/s}$$

neglecting minor corrections whose signs and magnitudes remain uncertain and which will largely cancel one another.

## II. Comparison of Mass Ratios Across Three Mission Architectures

Under the mandatory condition that proportional passive structures are consumed as combustible reaction mass (serial staging with self-consuming structures, as detailed in Chapter 5), we compare the required mass ratios $n$ for completing interplanetary missions across three distinct operational profiles:

### 1. Pure Propulsive Braking (No Atmospheric Deceleration)
If the rocket does not utilize atmospheric drag upon returning to Earth, relying entirely upon retro-rocket thrust to extinguish return velocity, the total mission characteristic velocity reaches $W = 24,000\ \text{m/s}$. The required mass ratio $n$ then swells to astronomical figures:
* **Flight into interplanetary space and direct return to Earth without landing**: $n = 120$;
* **Landing on the lunar surface and returning to Earth**: $n = 1000$;
* **Landing on the Martian surface and returning to Earth**: $n = 3000$!

### 2. First Atmospheric Deceleration Mode (Circular Orbit Glide, Reducing Active Velocity to $14,460\ \text{m/s}$)
If the first atmospheric deceleration method described in Chapter 9 is adopted, reducing the active velocity required of the rocket to $W = 14,460\ \text{m/s}$, the required mass ratio $n$ across all mission profiles is **reduced by a factor of six (a 6-fold reduction)**:
* **Earth-interplanetary round trip**: $n_E = 20$;
* **Lunar landing and return to Earth**: $n_m = 160$;
* **Martian expedition and return to Earth**: $n_M = 500$.

### 3. Second Atmospheric Deceleration Mode (Multi-Pass Aerobraking, Reducing Active Velocity to $12,500\ \text{m/s}$)
If the second atmospheric deceleration method described in Chapter 9 (multi-pass grazing aerobraking) is implemented, the required active rocket velocity drops further to $W = 12,500\ \text{m/s}$. The mass ratio is then **staggeringly reduced by a factor of twelve (a 12-fold reduction)** *(Editor's Note: See Note 16)*:
* **Earth-interplanetary round trip**: $n_E = 10$;
* **Lunar landing and return to Earth**: $n_m = 80$;
* **Martian expedition and return to Earth**: $n_M = 250$!

This dramatic reduction in mass ratio $n$ permits incorporating a substantial fraction of inexpensive hydrocarbon fuels into the propellant formulation, or drastically curtailing the quantity of structural deadweight that must be crushed and pulverized for combustion during transit.

---

## III. The Multiplier Effect of the Interplanetary Intermediate Base

If the aforementioned deep-space exploratory voyages depart directly from the **"Interplanetary Intermediate Base" (lunar orbit space station)** described in Chapter 10, the required mass ratio $n$ is **further reduced by a factor of 4 to 5** relative to the values above:
* **One-way cargo delivery from the base to the lunar surface**: $n = 4$;
* **Lunar landing and return to base (lunar round trip)**: $n_m = 15$;
* **Martian expedition and return to base (Mars round trip)**: $n_M = 45$.

Delivering supplies from Earth's surface to the lunar orbit base using pure rocket propulsion costs approximately four times as much per unit payload as transporting supplies from the base to the lunar surface. However, if the **"mountain-tunnel gun + secondary rocket" composite launch system** detailed in Chapter 10 is employed, the propellant mass and expenditure required to supply the base are cut by more than half!

---

## IV. Technical Feasibility: Moon vs. Mars Expeditions

Contrasting the Moon and Mars—the two primary target worlds:
1. **Gravitational acceleration and orbital velocity**: Surface gravity on Mars is only about half that of Earth (actually $\approx 0.38\, g$), and Mars' circular orbital velocity $w_M$ is less than half of Earth's;
2. **Aerodynamic gliding conditions**: Mars possesses an atmosphere; consequently, the lift-to-drag performance of a gliding entry vehicle in the Martian atmosphere is exceptionally favorable;
3. **Payload delivery economics**: Utilizing aerodynamic drag in the Martian atmosphere to dissipate arrival velocity for a winged landing, the propellant cost per unit payload delivered to the Martian surface **is approximately equal to that required for delivery to the airless lunar surface**!

Compared to a lunar landing, the true technical challenges of a Martian expedition do not lie in dynamic or mass ratio barriers, but reside entirely in:
* **The prolonged mission duration**: A round-trip transit cannot require less than six months;
* **Massive life-support logistics**: The colossal tonnage of oxygen, potable water, rations, and power consumables required to sustain the crew during extended interplanetary transit;
* **Uncertain surface environments** and precautionary measures regarding hypothetical Martian biological hazards.
