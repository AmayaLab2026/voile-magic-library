# Appendix · Academic Commentary and Editor's Annotations to "Conquest of Interplanetary Space"

## I. Historical Publication Background

Yuriy Vasil'yevich Kondratyuk's monumental treatise *Conquest of Interplanetary Space* (*Завоевание межпланетных пространств*) was published in early 1929 in Novosibirsk, Siberia, as a private edition financed entirely at the author's personal expense.

However, the underlying research and manuscript drafting had been initiated more than a decade earlier. According to Kondratyuk's personal records, the core physical concepts and mathematical derivations were formulated between 1917 and 1919. In 1925, while working in the Kuban region, Kondratyuk systematically expanded and revised the manuscript. In the spring of 1926, the work was dispatched to Moscow and submitted for review to the eminent master of Russian aerodynamics and aeronautical mechanics, **Professor Vladimir Petrovich Vetchinkin (Prof. V. P. Vetchinkin)** of the Zhukovsky Air Force Engineering Academy.

After thoroughly studying the manuscript, Professor Vetchinkin composed a glowing formal academic review in April 1926.

Despite Professor Vetchinkin's passionate recommendation, the Soviet Main Scientific Board (Glavnauka) not only refused to allocate publishing funds, but declined even to render administrative assistance. Confronted with bureaucratic inertia, Kondratyuk was forced to save from his modest wages as a grain elevator design engineer, finally paying a local Novosibirsk print shop to typeset and print a few thousand copies of his epoch-making astronautical treatise.

In 1947, following Kondratyuk's heroic sacrifice on the frontlines of the Great Patriotic War, the State Publishing House of the Defense Industry (Oborongiz) republished the work under the meticulous editorship of **Petr Ivanovich Ivanov (P. I. Ivanov)**, incorporating extensive technical corrections and scholarly commentary. In the present collection, the text is reproduced in full according to the 1929 first edition, omitting only the concluding list of mathematical notations, while gathering below all the essential annotations of Professor Vetchinkin (from the 1929 edition) and P. I. Ivanov (from the 1947 Oborongiz edition).

## II. Review Report on the Manuscript of Yu. Kondratyuk by Mechanical Engineer Prof. V. P. Vetchinkin (April 1926)

**Report on the Review of the Manuscript by Yu. Kondratyuk, "On Interplanetary Travel" (*О межпланетных путешествиях*)**  
*Reviewer: Mechanical Engineer, Professor of the Moscow Zhukovsky Air Force Engineering Academy V. P. Vetchinkin (April 1926)*

### 1. Evaluation of Independent Investigation and Originality
The author candidly states in his preface that when undertaking this investigation, he was completely unacquainted with the recent works of foreign scientists in this field, and had not even had access to the foundational classical treatises of our own countryman, K. E. Tsiolkovskiy.

Nevertheless, this profound isolation in no way hindered the author from independently deriving and arriving at all the principal scientific conclusions established by earlier theorists of interplanetary flight—a fact that in itself bears eloquent testimony to the author's exceptional scientific acumen and persevering tenacity!

Simultaneously, the completely unique expository style adopted throughout the work, together with a non-standard mathematical nomenclature distinct from conventional academic usage, demonstrates beyond dispute that **Mechanical Engineer Yu. Kondratyuk thought through and composed this brilliant work completely autonomously, free from any external scientific influence!**

On the basis of these facts, there is every justification to affirm that this monograph represents the brilliant outcome of entirely independent scientific investigation.

### 2. Chapter-by-Chapter Academic Assessment
We now examine the scientific contents of the manuscript in detail:

* **Chapter I (Definitions)**: Sets forth basic definitions and physical concepts concerning the rocket, payload, and interplanetary flight. These definitions are fully correct, though expressed in an idiosyncratic style bearing the author's distinctive personal imprint.
* **Chapter II (Propulsion Fundamentals and Exhaust Velocity)**: Investigates reactive propulsion principles. The author derives governing formulas for determining the expenditure of reaction mass and analyzes the energetic efficiency of combustion. His conclusions are entirely rigorous, although non-standard notations slightly impede rapid comprehension.
* **Chapter III (Staging and Combustible Structures)**: The author investigates dividing the rocket into independent operational stages to minimize overall mass ratio. Here he introduces a profoundly original engineering concept: **manufacturing proportional passive structures (such as propellant tanks) from metallic materials capable of secondary combustion, feeding them directly into the combustion chamber as fuel once the primary propellants are exhausted**. An algebraic typographical error is evident in formula (6) on manuscript page 15 (page 72 of the collected edition): the denominator should be written as $1 - q(n-1)$ rather than the form appearing in the handwritten copy.
* **Chapter IV (Combustion Chambers and Nozzle Structures)**: The author analyzes in detail the combustion process within the chamber, expansion dynamics along the de Laval nozzle, and the fluid mechanical and thermodynamic conditions required for stable chamber operation under high pressure and in vacuum (Note: this chapter was composed specifically prior to the 1929 publication following review recommendations).
* **Chapter V (Trajectories and Orbital Transfers)**: Investigates diverse flight trajectories, tangential transfer orbits between trajectories, characteristic velocity increments required for these maneuvers, and feasible trajectories for departing from and returning to Earth. The exposition is highly original; all dynamic derivations are **completely correct**! In analyzing return braking trajectories, Kondratyuk surpassed all previously published literature by independently proposing **equipping the rocket with lifting surfaces to execute an aerodynamic gliding descent through the atmosphere**—a concept conceived independently and simultaneously by F. A. Tsander.
* **Chapter VI (Acceleration Tolerance)**: Examines the physiological capacity of the human body to sustain prolonged high accelerations (mechanical overload). The author's assessments of physiological tolerance thresholds are sound and reasonable.
* **Chapter VII (Atmospheric Effects on Rocket Flight)**: Analyzes aerodynamic drag and heating. Beyond conventional drag, the author undertakes a pioneering investigation of severe aerodynamic surface heating caused by intense adiabatic shock compression at hypersonic velocities, formulating the principle of radiative thermal protection via fourth-power emission into space (Stefan-Boltzmann law).
* **Chapter VIII (Dissipation of Return Velocity by Atmospheric Drag)**: Investigates aerodynamic braking to dissipate second cosmic velocity upon planetary return. The author displays astonishing engineering insight: by designing a specialized glider with variable-incidence wings and utilizing multi-pass skimming trajectories through the upper atmosphere, the vast bulk of re-entry kinetic energy is dissipated safely in the air, slashing the required propulsive retro-burn from $11\ \text{km/s}$ to a few hundred meters per second. This constitutes one of the most brilliant contributions of the monograph.
* **Chapter IX (Interplanetary Bases and Rocket-Artillery Systems)**: The author proposes establishing a permanent artificial satellite base in lunar orbit (the direct theoretical forerunner of the renowned **Lunar Orbit Rendezvous / LOR architecture**), and proposes employing an ultra-long ground gun to launch unmanned freight containers to drastically cut logistics costs.
* **Chapter X (Rocket Control and Instrumentation)**: Explores automatic flight controls, gyroscopic stabilization, jet vanes inserted into the rocket exhaust, and dedicated celestial navigation instruments. The conceptual trajectory of the author's control architecture is entirely sound, though shop-level mechanical construction drawings remain to be developed.
* **Chapter XI (General Perspectives and Lunar/Martian Expeditions)**: Surveys future horizons of interplanetary flight, calculating engineering parameters not only for circumterrestrial, circumlunar, and lunar landing missions, but for expeditions to Mars. Although dynamic mass ratio conditions for Mars are almost as favorable as for the Moon (owing to aerodynamic gliding in the Martian atmosphere), I personally regard manned flight to Mars as somewhat premature at present—because a mission duration exceeding six months entails carrying massive life-support logistics. Beyond this caution, the author cannot be reproached for ungrounded fantasy.
* **Chapter XII (Outline of Essential Experimental Work)**: Details ten vital scientific experiments that must be performed prior to fabricating manned interplanetary spacecraft. Covering engine test-stand firings, high-energy propellant synthesis, human g-tolerance, upper-atmosphere sounding, and supersonic thermal glide tests, this program is remarkably realistic and scientific.

### 3. General Conclusion and Recommendation for Publication
The manuscript of Comrade Kondratyuk **fully attains the highest academic level and warrants immediate publication!**

In view of the exceptional original talent displayed by the author, state scientific institutions should make every effort to acquire all preserved manuscript records from the author and provide him with all necessary research facilities, enabling his extraordinary gifts to be dedicated entirely to the advancement of Soviet aerospace science.

## III. Complete Academic Annotations by P. I. Ivanov (1947 Oborongiz Edition, Notes 1–16)

The following annotations were prepared by editor P. I. Ivanov for the 1947 edition of *Conquest of Interplanetary Space* issued by the State Publishing House of the Defense Industry (Oborongiz):

### Note 1 (Corresponding to page 62 of text)
In reality, when the ratio of single-stage velocity increment to exhaust velocity satisfies $W_i / u \ll 1$, the stage mass ratio $n_i = e^{W_i/u}$ can be expanded in the first two terms of a Taylor series:

$$n_i = e^{\frac{W_i}{u}} \approx 1 + \frac{W_i}{u}$$

Substituting this two-term expansion into the payload ratio $i = M_f / M_0$ directly yields the first-order linear approximation presented in the text.

### Note 2 (Corresponding to page 63 of text)
This derivation rests upon the fundamental assumption that the effective exhaust velocity $u$ of the rocket motor remains constant throughout the firing duration.

### Note 3 (Corresponding to page 64 of text)
The concept of admixing dense solid powders or liquid droplets into the gaseous exhaust jet to increase reactionary momentum appears attractive at first glance. However, in physical reality, injecting condensed particles into a high-speed gas stream induces substantial velocity degradation of the gaseous working fluid due to two-phase gas-solid friction and thermodynamic non-equilibrium. Consequently, only under highly specific propellant specific impulses and nozzle profiles can particle-loading yield a net momentum benefit.

### Note 4 (Corresponding to page 66 of text)
The author defines $W_1$ as twice the parabolic escape velocity from Earth's surface (second cosmic velocity), assuming zero initial velocity at the surface and a geocentric focal trajectory:

$$W_1 = 2\, W_p = 2\sqrt{2 g R} = 2 \times 11,185\ \text{m/s} = 22,370\ \text{m/s}$$

where $R$ is Earth's radius and $g$ is sea-level gravitational acceleration. The author's $W_2$ represents the difference between escape velocity $W_1$ and first cosmic velocity (circular orbital speed) $W_c = \sqrt{g R} = 7910\ \text{m/s}$:

$$W_2 = W_1 - W_c = 11,185 - 7910 = 3275\ \text{m/s}$$

The coefficient preceding 11,185 is derived rigorously from the gravitational potential integral.

### Note 5 (Corresponding to page 68 of text)
The author's emphasis on elevating combustion chamber pressure is thermodynamically sound: increasing the initial combustion pressure enhances the expansion work in the de Laval nozzle, thereby raising the thermal cycle efficiency. In engineering practice, however, this must be balanced against the increased structural mass of chamber walls required to contain ultra-high pressures, as well as the added mass of high-pressure turbopump machinery.

### Note 6 (Corresponding to page 71 of text)
Equation (6) in the text can be expressed algebraically as:

$$\eta_1 = \frac{m(n-1)}{1 - q(n-1)}$$

Hence, if the structural consumption coefficient satisfies $q \ll \frac{1}{n-1}$, the effective mass ratio $\eta_1$ closely approaches the theoretical limit $m(n-1)$. As $q$ increases, under continuous operation throughout stage $m_1$, the difference tends toward zero.

### Note 7 (Corresponding to page 71 of text)
The author notes here that by feeding proportional passive structure $m_1$ into the engine as auxiliary fuel, the final burnout velocity increment can be **fully doubled (increased by 100%)** compared to conventional staging where empty structures are jettisoned without combustion ($m_1 = 0$).

### Note 8 (Corresponding to page 71 of text)
The inequality constraint $q \ll \frac{1}{5(n_i - 1)}$ demonstrates that selecting structural consumption parameter $q$ according to this criterion ensures that the effective velocity delivered by each stage in a series-staged rocket remains strictly proportional to the theoretical optimum.

Regarding mass distribution across $K$ stages, variational optimization using Lagrange multipliers rigorously proves that **the total lift-off mass is minimized if and only if the individual mass ratios of all stages are strictly equal ($n_1 = n_2 = \dots = n_K = \sqrt[K]{n}$)**. As the number of stages $K$ increases, the actual mass ratio approaches the theoretical continuum envelope.

### Note 9 (Corresponding to page 71 of text)
Because the stage mass ratio is defined as $n_i = M_{i0} / M_{if}$, and the dry burnout mass $M_{if}$ already incorporates the structural fraction supporting the subsequent $(i+1)$-th stage, analyzing the limiting condition $n_i = 1$ is mechanically and physically meaningful.

### Note 10 (Corresponding to page 72 of text)
To derive the characteristic numerical values given by Kondratyuk (namely $q = 1/9$ for a two-stage system and $q = 1/3.9$ for a three-stage system), it must be recognized that the author assigns each stage to an independent propulsion unit and specifies equal velocity increments $W_i$.

For a single-stage system, $W_1 = W$; for a two-stage system, each stage delivers $W_i = W/2$, giving stage mass ratios $n_i = \sqrt{n}$. For multi-stage architectures, equating the structural mass ratios gives:

$$\frac{m(n-1)}{1 - q(n-1)} = \sum_{i=1}^K \frac{m(n_i - 1)}{1 - q(n_i - 1)}$$

Solving this transcendental equation yields $q = 1/9 = 0.111$ for a two-stage vehicle, and $q = 1/3.9 = 0.256$ for a three-stage vehicle. Kondratyuk's engineering calculation is entirely exact!

### Note 11 (Corresponding to page 72 of text)
The propellant mass distribution here corresponds rigorously to the conservation of momentum equations for sequential staging with jettisoned tankage.

### Note 12 (Corresponding to page 74 of text)
The characteristic velocity equation cited by the author:

$$W_e = v (\sqrt{2} - 1)$$

is derived under the boundary condition that the rocket, having ascended vertically through the dense atmosphere, accelerates tangentially in near-Earth space to inject into a parabolic escape orbit. Here tangential circular velocity is $v = \sqrt{g R}$ and parabolic escape velocity is $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2 g R}$, the difference being precisely $(\sqrt{2}-1)v$.

### Note 13 (Corresponding to pages 80–84 of text, Core Analytical Proof)
The central trajectory dynamic equation (12) in the text is derived rigorously from differential equation (9) through the following mathematical formulation:

Let total vehicle acceleration be $j$, where $j = J_0 + J_p$, taking gravitational acceleration as approximately constant $g = g_0$. Resolving equations of motion along the tangential and normal trajectory coordinates:

$$\frac{dv}{dt} = j - g \sin \theta \quad (A)$$

$$v \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \left( \frac{v^2}{r} - g \right) \cos \theta \quad (B)$$

Under Kondratyuk's condition of **horizontal acceleration at constant orbital altitude**, trajectory inclination $\theta = 0$, and orbital radius $r = R$ (Earth's radius). Normal acceleration balance requires aerodynamic lift plus centrifugal force to balance gravity:

$$j_p = g \left(1 - \frac{v^2}{w_c^2}\right)$$

where $w_c = \sqrt{g R}$ is circular orbital speed. Geometrically, resultant engine thrust acceleration is:

$$j = \sqrt{J_0^2 + J_p^2}$$

Integrating along flight time to solve for total thrust work $L$, we expand the radical via binomial series under the high thrust-to-drag assumption $\delta_c = J_0 / g \gg 1$:

$$\sqrt{1 + \frac{J_p^2}{J_0^2}} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \frac{J_p^2}{J_0^2} - \frac{1}{8} \frac{J_p^4}{J_0^4} + \dots$$

Integrating over velocity yields the closed-form expression for velocity loss:

$$L = \frac{1}{g} \frac{1}{2(\delta_c^2 - 1)}$$

This fully confirms the mathematical self-consistency and dynamic rigor of text equation (12).

### Note 14 (Corresponding to page 85 of text)
Extensive centrifuge trials in modern aerospace medicine demonstrate that **when subjects are positioned in a supine posture facing backward relative to the acceleration vector, human tolerance to longitudinal acceleration vastly exceeds Kondratyuk's conservative estimates**. In the chest-to-back ($+\text{Gx}$) physiological posture, trained astronauts safely sustain severe accelerations of $8 \sim 12\, g$ for several minutes without blackout or loss of consciousness.

### Note 15 (Corresponding to page 112 of text)
The theoretical exhaust velocity $u = 4700\ \text{m/s}$ assumed by the author was overly optimistic relative to chemical rocket capabilities of that era.

For conventional hydrocarbon fuels (such as kerosene and liquid oxygen), practical exhaust velocities at typical chamber pressures and sea-level expansion ratios range from $2800$ to $3100\ \text{m/s}$; even for the liquid hydrogen/liquid oxygen combination, the theoretical vacuum exhaust velocity ceiling is approximately $4200 \sim 4400\ \text{m/s}$. To achieve exhaust velocities exceeding $4700\ \text{m/s}$, one must employ exotic high-energy working media such as atomic hydrogen or ozone/liquid fluorine.

### Note 16 (Corresponding to page 113 of text)
Here:
* $n_E$ designates the overall mission mass ratio for departing Earth and returning safely;
* $n_m$ designates the overall mission mass ratio for a lunar expedition landing on the Moon and returning to Earth;
* $n_M$ designates the overall mission mass ratio for an expedition to Mars, landing on the Martian surface and returning to Earth.
