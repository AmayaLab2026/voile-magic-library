# Editor's Preface: Pioneers of Rocket Technology and the Path to the Conquest of Space
### Editor's Preface by T. M. Mel'kumov

> **“...Socialism is the reliable launching pad from which the Soviet Union sends into the boundless spaces of the universe its powerful and ever more perfect spaceships.”**  
> — *Pravda*, August 16, 1962

---

In our time, science and technology have achieved monumental successes in the grand endeavor of conquering outer space—successes that have captivated the entire world. Yet these magnificent achievements did not arrive by chance, nor were they accidental strokes of fortune; they represent the culmination of decades of tireless, self-sacrificing intellectual labor by scores of scientists, master designers, and inventors.

During the Second World War, the truck-mounted Soviet gunpowder tactical rocket weapon—the "Katyusha"—entered combat; German armed forces deployed the A-4 ballistic rocket (better known to the world as the V-2) to bombard London, a vehicle with a launch weight of 13 tons and an operational range of up to 350 kilometers. In the pre-war years and the early period of the war, humanity witnessed the initial crucial flights of liquid-propellant rocket engines.

An in-depth analysis of the historical evolution of rocketry revealed the extraordinary, colossal potential inherent in the rocket. Shortly after the war, this potential was brilliantly confirmed: starting in 1949, in the Soviet Union and other nations, sounding rockets and meteorological rockets equipped with specialized scientific instrument packages were launched into the upper atmosphere to investigate atmospheric composition, density, and temperature profiles. Soviet rocketry pioneered dedicated biological test capsules, sending experimental dogs to high altitudes and safely recovering them via specially engineered parachute systems.

It became increasingly evident that the rocket was not merely an instrument of military application; far more significantly, it held the **crucial key enabling humanity to comprehend the boundless cosmos beyond Earth and achieve travel to other planets of the solar system**. Focused on this noble objective, the Soviet Union embarked on a massive, planned program to develop the complex engineering systems required for the exploration of outer space.

---

## From Artificial Satellites and Planetary Probes to Manned Spaceflight

**October 4, 1957**, stands universally recognized as a historic milestone in human civilization: in the Soviet Union, the world's first artificial Earth satellite ("Sputnik") was successfully placed into orbit. From that juncture onward, humanity initiated systematic exploration of the vacuum environment surrounding the Earth through a sequence of increasingly sophisticated satellites.

One technological frontier after another was breached:
* Humanity created the first artificial solar asteroid;
* The Soviet pennant landed on the surface of the Moon;
* An automatic interplanetary space station executed the first lunar flyby, photographing and transmitting back to Earth the very first clear, historic images of the Moon's enigmatic far side;
* Automatic interplanetary stations were launched toward Venus;
* A series of satellite test flights carrying live biological specimens (animals and plants) was successfully conducted, culminating in precise recoveries within designated landing zones on Earth.

These systematic scientific investigations conducted in the Soviet Union amassed vast quantities of invaluable scientific data, blazing a broad trail for human beings themselves to journey into outer space.

On **April 12, 1961**, human history opened a new epoch. On that day, the 4,725-kilogram Soviet manned spaceship *Vostok*, piloted by the world's first cosmonaut, **Yuriy Alekseyevich Gagarin**, completed a historic orbital flight around the globe. After circling the Earth, Gagarin landed safely in the designated recovery area in excellent physical and mental condition.

On August 6–7, 1961, Cosmonaut G. S. Titov, piloting *Vostok-2* (weighing 4,731 kilograms), executed an extended 25-hour spaceflight, completing 17 orbits around the Earth and covering a distance exceeding 700,000 kilometers (roughly equivalent to a round-trip journey to the Moon).

On August 11, 1962, a new multi-ton Soviet spaceship, *Vostok-3*, carried Cosmonaut A. G. Nikolayev into orbit. A day later, on August 12, another Soviet spacecraft, *Vostok-4*, launched Cosmonaut P. R. Popovich into space, entering an orbit calculated to bring it into close proximity with *Vostok-3*, where the two executed the first coordinated group flight in space history. For the first time, two human beings simultaneously inhabited outer space, flying in tandem, maintaining continuous radio contact with ground control as well as clear ship-to-ship voice communications. This dual-spacecraft group mission, whose precision of organization astonished the world, lasted approximately 71 hours. *Vostok-3* logged 95 hours in orbit, completing 64 revolutions over 2.6 million kilometers; *Vostok-4* logged 71 hours, completing 48 revolutions over 2 million kilometers.

Approximately one year later, Soviet cosmonauts executed another joint mission. On June 14, 1963, *Vostok-5*, piloted by V. F. Bykovskiy, took to orbit; two days later, on June 16, *Vostok-6* was launched, carrying **the world's first female cosmonaut, V. V. Tereshkova**. Bykovskiy's flight endured for 119 hours, completing 95 revolutions around the Earth and covering over 3.3 million kilometers; *Vostok-6* spent 71 hours in orbit, completing 48 revolutions over 2 million kilometers.

The entire world bore witness to the outstanding engineering reliability of Soviet launch vehicle systems, the immense power of their rocket propulsion plants, and the high degree of refinement in both the engineering and life-support subsystems of the spacecraft. Throughout the flights, during which the cosmonauts experienced complete weightlessness, their vital physiological indicators remained entirely within normal parameters. Specialized ground training with advanced simulators, combined with physical conditioning, irrefutably demonstrated the sound methodology developed by medical and biological specialists for long-duration spaceflights. During their missions, cosmonauts unstrapped from their couches, recorded instrument readings, and captured motion pictures of the Earth and the Moon.

The tasks of dispatching cosmonauts into lunar orbit, landing on the lunar surface, and flying to neighboring planets were formally placed on humanity's scientific agenda. On February 12, 1961, the Soviet Union launched an automatic interplanetary station toward Venus; on November 1, 1962, another automatic station was launched into the vicinity of Mars.

Following the Soviet Union, **the United States also entered the field of space exploration**. The United States launched numerous satellites for diverse applications. In February 1962, as part of Project Mercury, the United States orbited a spacecraft carrying Astronaut John Glenn. Subsequently, three more manned orbital missions were conducted, culminating in the flight of Astronaut Gordon Cooper (22 orbits, lasting 34 hours). In 1962, the United States launched the Mariner-2 automatic interplanetary probe toward Venus.

Public interest in rocket systems and spacecraft surged on an unprecedented scale. Humanity turned its gaze toward distant worlds, fully confident that its boldest dreams would be transformed into reality.

## Tracing Theoretical Origins: The Great Russian Pioneering Thinkers

In this era of magnificent space achievements, one naturally looks back with deep reverence to the foundational sources of astronautical theory and spacecraft concepts. The historical spotlight shines with unmistakable clarity on several preeminent pioneers of our motherland:
* **Constantine Eduardovich Tsiolkovskiy (1857–1935)**
* **Fridrikh Arturovich Tsander (1887–1933)**
* **Yuriy Vasil'yevich Kondratyuk (1897–1942)**

Among these celebrated names of Russian pioneers who established the theory of spaceflight, one must also add the Russian revolutionary **Nikolay Ivanovich Kibal'chich (born in 1853)**. Sentenced to death in 1881 for his involvement in the assassination of Tsar Alexander II, he produced in his solitary death row cell, on the eve of his execution, an engineering manuscript with text and drawings proposing a reactive flying machine. Kibal'chich's manuscript was locked away in the secret archives of the Tsarist Police Department, remaining hidden until its rediscovery following the victory of the 1917 Russian Revolution; this precious manuscript appropriately opens the present collection.

The Soviet people feel justifiable pride not only because their homeland was the first to launch artificial satellites, interplanetary stations, and the manned *Vostok* spacecraft, but also because the foundational theoretical tenets of interplanetary flight were laid down by Tsiolkovskiy at a time when humanity was still debating whether heavier-than-air airplanes could fly at all.

### Tsiolkovskiy's Pioneering Contributions
Tsiolkovskiy's epoch-making initial treatise was published in 1903 in issue No. 5 of *Nauchnoye Obozreniye* (*Scientific Review*), entitled *Investigation of Cosmic Space by Means of Reaction Devices* (*Issledovaniye mirovykh prostranstv reaktivnymi priborami*); the underlying theoretical inquiries had begun in 1896. In 1911 and 1912, he published continuation papers in *Vestnik Vozdukhoplavaniya* (*Aeronautical Herald*), issues No. 19–22 and 2–9.

These treatises contained remarkably bold, original ideas along with the first analytic mathematical equations essential for understanding the principal factors governing spacecraft flight. Tsiolkovskiy derived the famous ideal velocity equation connecting maximum vehicle velocity to exhaust velocity and the initial-to-final mass ratio. He focused intensely on high-energy rocket propellants, asserting that outdated gunpowder rockets must be replaced by liquid-propellant rocket engines, specifically advocating liquid oxygen as the oxidizer and liquid hydrogen or petroleum hydrocarbons as fuel. Tsiolkovskiy proposed regenerative cooling—using one of the liquid propellant components to cool the combustion chamber walls exposed to extremely high combustion temperatures. To control flight attitude in the vacuum of space, he proposed placing jet vanes (gas rudders) in the rocket exhaust plume—a concept first brought to engineering fruition half a century later in the German V-2 rocket.

Tsiolkovskiy consistently analyzed problems from the long-term perspective of interplanetary voyages. He clearly demonstrated the advantages and feasibility of establishing interplanetary stations in Earth orbit, and foresaw the immense impetus artificial satellites would provide to astronomical observation. It is not our intent in this preface to summarize all of Tsiolkovskiy's rich ideas in rocketry and astronautics; readers are directed to his original works. Admittedly, certain specific conceptions and philosophical prophecies regarding humanity's distant future have proven inaccurate or erroneous today. Nevertheless, it must be remembered that his principal works were composed in the pre-revolutionary era, when scientific theory and experimental capabilities were far more rudimentary than today. This in no way diminishes Tsiolkovskiy's universally recognized status as the founding father of rocket engineering and astronautics.

### Tsander's Theoretical and Engineering Practice
F. A. Tsander was a passionate advocate and pioneer of the engineering realization of interplanetary flight. He began publishing in 1924. On the theoretical plane, his inquiries into interplanetary flight and rocket propulsion cut deeper than those of any contemporary; furthermore, as a highly trained mechanical engineer, he tackled specific structural design challenges of propulsion powerplants and spacecraft hulls. Tsander conducted systematic experimental programs on jet engine models and rocket test stands.

Recognizing the vital necessity of closed-loop life support systems during extended interplanetary journeys, Tsander systematically conducted experiments on soilless plant cultivation, notably cultivating citrus trees using charcoal granules saturated with nutrient solutions. Among Tsander's many original concepts, particular emphasis must be placed on:
* An internal combustion engine running on liquid oxygen and gasoline, completely independent of atmospheric oxygen;
* The concept of the self-consuming rocket, wherein structural metal components no longer needed during flight are melted down and fed into the combustion chamber as supplemental high-energy metallic fuel;
* The theory of radiative cooling of spacecraft hulls during aerodynamic gliding reentry into planetary atmospheres;
* Hypersonic thermal protection systems for atmospheric reentry;
* Rigorous thermodynamic efficiency calculations for diverse engine cycles (including air-breathing and combined-cycle systems), as well as calculus-based trajectory optimization for interplanetary transfers.

### Kondratyuk's Independent Theoretical Insights
Among the broader public, Yuriy Vasil'yevich Kondratyuk has been relatively lesser known. His mathematical and theoretical approach differed fundamentally from Tsiolkovskiy's. In his manuscript *To Whoever Will Read to Build* (*Tem, kto budet chitat', chtoby stroit'*, established by archival research to have been composed between 1918 and 1919, reproduced in Part 2 of this collection), he articulated a remarkable series of profound engineering concepts.

Specifically, Kondratyuk discussed:
* The possibility of propelling space rockets using directed streams of energetic particles (such as $\alpha$-particles or charged microscopic particles);
* "Two-axis astatic gyroscopes" for maneuvering and attitude stabilization;
* A **checkerboard array layout** for fuel and oxidizer injectors—a configuration that remains ubiquitous in modern liquid rocket engine injector faces today;
* Mechanical integrating instruments for calculating instantaneous spacecraft velocity and propellant consumption;
* Pioneer thermal protection schemes for safe atmospheric reentry, utilizing internal convective cooling circuits or jettisonable ablative/insulating outer shells;
* Electric propulsion concepts using solar radiation to heat a working medium or generate electrical power for cathode-ray particle thrusters during low-thrust interplanetary cruise;
* And, echoing Tsiolkovskiy, the establishment of intermediate orbital supply depots, specifically including lunar orbital stations.

We must, however, direct the reader's attention to the cautionary remarks in our editorial commentary at the end of this volume: because this manuscript was not published during Kondratyuk's lifetime, there is insufficient documentary evidence to confirm that every idea therein can be strictly dated to 1918–1919, nor can we guarantee that every view originated solely with the author. Building upon this manuscript, Kondratyuk greatly expanded and revised his work, self-publishing his magnum opus *Conquest of Interplanetary Space* (*Zavoyevaniye mezhplanetnykh prostranstv*) in 1929. This landmark work is likewise reproduced in full in Part 3 of this volume.

Today, against the backdrop of the Soviet Union's triumphant space achievements, the attentive reader can appreciate the astonishing intellectual daring required of Tsiolkovskiy, Tsander, and Kondratyuk. In an era when humanity was still pondering whether primitive biplanes could sustain flight, these pioneers dared to project their thoughts into the remote cosmos, anchoring their visions with rigorous scientific theory and engineering mechanics. While reading these archival works, we readily understand the occasional inaccuracies inevitable under the historical constraints of their era, and we pay our profoundest homage to their immeasurable contributions to astronautics.

## State Support and Generational Continuity

It is deeply significant and historical that **the leader who first comprehended and steadfastly supported the pioneers of rocket technology was V. I. Lenin**. In the fledgling days of Soviet power, amidst economic devastation, civil war, and famine, Lenin, despite round-the-clock state affairs, displayed keen interest in the concept of interplanetary travel. He provided enormous moral encouragement to the work of F. A. Tsander. In 1921, the Council of People's Commissars of the RSFSR, "in recognition of the exceptional services rendered by the scientist, inventor, and aeronautical specialist K. E. Tsiolkovskiy," granted him a lifelong state pension, freeing him from everyday material burdens to devote himself entirely to astronautical dynamics and rocket research.

It was during these formative years of the Soviet state that the institutional foundations were laid, enabling victorious socialism to erect the magnificent edifice of Soviet rocket science and technology. The pioneering paths blazed by Tsiolkovskiy, Tsander, and Kondratyuk were taken up by generations of brilliant scientists, designers, theoreticians, and engineers whose boundless energy and devotion forged today's global renown for our motherland. The Soviet state provided visionary planning and comprehensive support for astronautical collectives. In our era, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, following Lenin's example, continues to exercise decisive leadership in advancing rocket technology. The Soviet people, in their march toward communism, rely on a robust modern industrial complex and cutting-edge science to continually chart new paths into the universe.

On May 1, 1933, listening to the Red May Day parade broadcast from Red Square over the radio, Tsiolkovskiy uttered this prophetic statement:
> **“For forty years I worked on the reactive engine, thinking that a flight to Mars would take hundreds of years. But times are changing. I am convinced that many of you will witness the first great flight beyond the atmosphere!”**

The pioneer's conviction sprang from his faith in the Bolshevik Party and the Soviet state as the true vanguard of human progress. Today, as that grand vision turns into triumphant reality, we honor the first generation of rocket specialists, scientists, and inventors who provided humanity with its initial wellspring of astronautical thought.

## Editorial Principles and Archival Sources of the Collection

This collection brings together the core foundational writings of Russian scientists—the pioneers of rocketry and astronautics—spanning the 1880s through the 1930s. Uniting these essential works into a single volume represents an undertaking of immense historical and scientific value. Many of these treatises had become exceedingly rare collector's items, while certain manuscripts remained scattered and inaccessible.

With the exception of Kondratyuk's manuscript *To Whoever Will Read to Build*, which is published here for the first time, most treatises in this collection were previously published more than once. However, in past reprintings, earlier editors frequently introduced unauthorized additions, omissions, and alterations. **In this standard academic edition by the Academy of Sciences, all texts are printed strictly according to the original versions published during the authors' lifetimes or from the original holographic manuscripts, faithfully preserving each author's distinctive vocabulary and stylistic characteristics**. Only orthography, typographical formats, and universally recognized standard abbreviations have been normalized, and obvious typographical errors corrected. All explanatory interpolations by the editorial board are strictly enclosed in square brackets `[ ]`.

Obscure, erroneous, or controversial formulations, as well as archaic Russian metrological units, are addressed in comprehensive editorial commentaries at the end of each section. These commentaries also include the authors' own latest revisions and additions found in proof sheets or subsequent working manuscripts. The commentaries have drawn extensively upon the evaluations of past scholarly editors of early editions.
