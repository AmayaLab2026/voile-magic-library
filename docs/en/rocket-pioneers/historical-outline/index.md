# Part 4: Pioneers of Rocket Technology (Historical Outline)

### Works of Russian Scientists — Pioneers of Rocket Technology (Historical Outline)
**V. N. Sokol'skiy**  
*Institute for the History of Natural Science and Technology, Academy of Sciences of the USSR / From NASA TT F-9285, pp. 128–158*

## Historical Introduction: Deep Roots of Russian Rocket Technology

The development of rocket technology in our country possesses a rich history spanning more than a century. Its origins reach back into the distant past: in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, Russian pyrotechnists, through their masterly workshop craft, thoroughly mastered the specialized techniques of manufacturing diverse signal and illumination rockets.

Entering the nineteenth century, military black powder rockets underwent large-scale engineering development and combat deployment in Russia. In the early nineteenth century, the distinguished Russian artillery expert **Aleksandr Dmitriyevich Zasyadko (1779–1837)** successfully developed a series of military field rockets and established the first specialized rocket manufacturing workshop in St. Petersburg.

In the mid-nineteenth century, one of the most prominent representatives of the Russian artillery school, **Konstantin Ivanovich Konstantinov (1817–1871)**, made epochal, foundational contributions to rocketry. Konstantinov systematically investigated the internal and external ballistics of black powder rockets and devised the world's first **ballistic pendulum** for recording the instantaneous thrust curves of rocket engines. He created a modern rocket plant in St. Petersburg, mechanized and standardized rocket mass production, developed field rocket weapon systems with ranges of 4 to 5 kilometers, and established regular rocket artillery batteries.

Until the middle of the nineteenth century, however, rocket development was confined almost exclusively to military domains (signaling, illumination, and artillery fire). It was not until the second half of the nineteenth century, with the emergence of aeronautics and advances in thermodynamics, that progressive Russian engineers and scientists began seriously investigating **the utilization of the reactive principle to achieve controlled human flight through the atmosphere and into outer space**.

## I. I. I. Treteskiy's Compressed Gas and Steam Jet Aerostat (1849)

The earliest documented concept for utilizing reactive thrust to achieve controlled flight of an aerial apparatus was independently formulated in 1849 by the Russian military engineering officer **Ivan Ivanovich Treteskiy (1821–1895)** while stationed in Tiflis (now Tbilisi), Georgia.

Treteskiy authored a detailed engineering report entitled *On Methods of Controlling Aerostats* (*О способах управлять аэростатами*) (this manuscript remained unpublished during his lifetime and is preserved in full in the Central State Military-Historical Archive of the USSR [TsGVIA], Record Group 1, Inventory 1, File 17474, pp. 35–140).

Treteskiy clearly pointed out that conventional mechanical propellers or sails were heavy and inefficient for propelling massive, lightweight aerostats. He therefore proposed **directly discharging streams of compressed gas or steam at high velocity to generate reactive thrust for the propulsion and directional control of balloons and dirigibles**.

In Treteskiy's design:
* A steam boiler generating high-pressure steam, or containers of highly compressed gas, were installed in the center of the aerostat gondola;
* Four symmetrically arranged steam pipes extended outward from the boiler toward the four orthogonal directions of the aerostat: forward, aft, port, and starboard;
* The ends of the pipes were fitted with convergent directional nozzles and quick-acting control valves;
* By manipulating the valves, the pilot could discharge steam aft to propel the craft forward, forward to brake aerodynamically, or to port or starboard to execute agile turns in the air.

On December 31, 1851, the Artillery Branch of the Military-Scientific Committee (*Zhurnal Artilleriyskogo otdeleniya Voyenno-uchenogo komiteta*) conducted a thorough and serious technical review of Treteskiy's project (File 17474, pp. 153–157). The review committee commended the originality of the project, affirming that the physical principle of jet propulsion for aerostats was entirely sound, though the excessive deadweight of high-pressure boilers and gas cylinders under the manufacturing technology of the day limited its immediate practical execution.

Treteskiy's concept stands as the **first systematically documented engineering project in history for the reactive control of an aeronautical vehicle**.

## II. N. M. Sokovnin's Naval Rigid Jet Airship and Thrust Equation (1866)

In 1866, Imperial Russian Navy Vice-Admiral **Nikolay Mikhaylovich Sokovnin (1811–1894)** published a monograph in St. Petersburg entitled *The Airship* (*Воздушный корабль*).

Sokovnin proposed a monumental rigid-shell jet-propelled airship:
* **Structural Dimensions**: Length of 50 meters, width of 25 meters, height of 42 meters, constructed of an airtight rigid hull made of ultra-thin tinplate, filled with hydrogen, with a total volume of 35,000 cubic meters;
* **Powerplant**: A 100-horsepower **Lenoir gas internal combustion engine** mounted in the center of the hull, driving a large centrifugal high-pressure blower;
* **Propulsion Mechanism**: The blower drew ambient air from outside the hull, pressurized it, and routed it through a four-way manifold to discharge channels at the front, rear, and sides of the ship;
* **Jet Thrust Equation**: For the first time in aeronautical history, Sokovnin applied the momentum theorem of fluid mechanics to derive a quantitative mathematical equation for the reactive thrust $R$:

$$R = \frac{Q \gamma}{g} v$$

where $Q$ is the volume of air discharged per unit time, $\gamma$ is the specific weight of air, $g$ is the acceleration of gravity, and $v$ is the relative discharge velocity of the air jet at the nozzle exit. Sokovnin's rigorous calculations demonstrated that, given a sufficiently high mass flow rate and exhaust velocity, the resulting reactive thrust would suffice to propel a multi-ton rigid airship through the air at cruising speeds of tens of kilometers per hour.

## III. N. I. Kibal'chich's Pulsed Gunpowder Rocket Flying Machine (1881)

In March 1881, the young Russian revolutionary of the *Narodnaya Volya* and brilliant explosives engineer **Nikolay Ivanovich Kibal'chich (1853–1881)**, awaiting execution in the death row solitary cell of the Peter and Paul Fortress in St. Petersburg, drew upon heroic willpower and scientific clarity to complete his manuscript entitled *Preliminary Project for an Aeronautical Machine* (*Предварительный проект воздухоплавательного прибора*) just days before mounting the scaffold.

Marking a qualitative leap from the buoyancy-dependent concepts of Treteskiy and Sokovnin, Kibal'chich conceived a **pure reaction-propelled heavier-than-air vehicle entirely independent of atmospheric aerostatic buoyancy**:
* **Levitation and Propulsion Principle**: Pure downward discharge of high-temperature, high-pressure combustion gases from a specially designed gunpowder rocket engine mounted beneath the central platform, generating direct reactive thrust to counteract vehicle weight and provide climb authority;
* **Continuous Combustion Feeder Mechanism**: To overcome the instantaneous burning of gunpowder, he designed a **programmed continuous feed mechanism** driven by clockwork, sequentially introducing cylindrical compressed powder candles into the center of a heavy cast-iron combustion chamber;
* **Thrust-Vector Attitude Control**: The combustion chamber was suspended in a two-axis gimbal ring; by tilting the nozzle thrust axis, the pilot could generate horizontal force components for steering and cruise translation.

The Tsarist Department of Police classified this visionary manuscript as a state secret, **locking it away in secret archives for 36 years** until the October Revolution, when it was finally published in 1918 in the historical journal *Byloye* (*The Past*). Kibal'chich is rightfully celebrated as the first pioneer-martyr to formulate a design for a manned solid-rocket flying apparatus.

## IV. S. S. Nezhdanovskiy's World-First Invention of Liquid Rocket Propellants (1880–1884)

Another profound, epochal discovery in Russian aeronautical history is inextricably linked to **Sergey Sergeyevich Nezhdanovskiy (1850–1940)**, an eminent Moscow mechanician and close associate of N. E. Zhukovskiy.

Western historiography long held that the liquid-propellant rocket was first conceived by Tsiolkovskiy in 1903 or Goddard in 1914. However, the original holographic research notebooks preserved in the Scientific Archives of the N. E. Zhukovskiy Museum in Moscow (notebooks from 1880–1884, Record Group Nos. 1079, 2990/1, 2990/2, and 2990/3) irrefutably document that: **between 1880 and 1884, Nezhdanovskiy was the first in the world to formulate and mathematically calculate rocket propulsion utilizing bipropellant liquid systems (liquid hydrocarbon fuel + liquid oxidizer)!**

In his 1880 entry, Nezhdanovskiy explicitly wrote:
> "According to the principle of patented technology, explosive reacting substances can be prepared by using two liquids which, upon mixing, produce an energetic exothermic reaction... One liquid may be kerosene, gasoline, light hydrocarbons, or rectified alcohol; the other oxidizing liquid may be nitric acid, fuming nitric acid, or highly concentrated liquid peroxides."

Nezhdanovskiy did not merely suggest liquid propellants; he executed remarkably advanced engineering calculations:
1. **Derivation of Exhaust Velocity and Theoretical Specific Impulse**: Based on two-phase thermodynamic gas expansion equations, he computed theoretical exhaust velocities for kerosene burning with nitric acid to yield steam, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen;
2. **Regenerative Combustion Chamber Cooling**: He proposed circulating a cold liquid propellant component through a double-walled chamber jacket prior to injection to absorb heat from the chamber walls;
3. **De Laval Expansion Nozzle Sketches**: He drafted convergent-divergent supersonic nozzle profiles, applying them to conceptual designs of jet-propelled gliders and reaction-driven helicopter models.

Nezhdanovskiy's secret notebooks prove that Russian scientific exploration in **liquid rocket propellants** preceded that of all other nations by two full decades!

## V. F. R. Geshvend's Steam Jet Biplane "Parolyot" (1887)

In 1887, Kiev civil engineer **Feodor Romanovich Geshvend (1839–1890)** published two monographs in Kiev: *General Principles for the Design of an Aeronautical Steamship (Parolyot)* (*Общее основание устройства воздухоплавательного парохода (паролета)*, April 1887) and its *Supplement*.

Geshvend conceived an extraordinarily modern jet-propelled biplane—the **"Parolyot"**:
* **Aerodynamic Configuration**: An enclosed, streamlined elliptical hard-shell metal fuselage with biplane wings providing 60 square meters of lifting surface area;
* **Powerplant**: A rapid-steaming high-pressure watertube boiler operating at 15 atmospheres, fired by kerosene fuel;
* **Multistage Jet Ejector**: Geshvend's most brilliant fluid invention—steam was not simply discharged into the atmosphere, but exhausted through a series of **concentric annular convergent-divergent ejector conduits**. The high-velocity steam jet entrained massive quantities of cold ambient air into the ejector, increasing the total exhaust mass flow rate several-fold, thereby achieving immense reactive thrust at high propulsive efficiency at moderate subsonic cruise speeds;
* **Rail Catapult Takeoff and High-Speed Cruise**: A four-wheeled takeoff trolley running along a dedicated dual-rail track was designed to launch the craft, with theoretical calculations projecting cruise speeds of 280 kilometers per hour ($280\ \text{km/h}$).

On March 16, 1888, the Main Engineering Administration of the Russian War Ministry convened an expert technical committee chaired by the prominent aeronautical mechanician M. M. Boreskov to review Geshvend's engineering drawings and thermodynamic calculations (TsGVIA, Record Group 808, Inventory 1, File 26, pp. 14–17). Although the committee concluded that the required power-to-weight ratio of the boiler exceeded contemporary metallurgical capabilities, it fully affirmed the high originality of his multistage ejector reactive propulsion system.

## VI. A. P. Fedorov's Reaction Flying Machine Operating Without Atmospheric Medium (1896)

In 1896, **Aleksandr Petrovich Fedorov** published a treatise in St. Petersburg entitled *A New Principle of Aeronautics Excluding the Atmosphere as a Supporting Medium* (*Новый принцип воздухоплавания, исключающий атмосферу как опорную среду*).

Fedorov theoretically demonstrated that aeronautical travel could be freed from reliance on atmospheric buoyancy (balloons) or aerodynamic wing lift (airplanes). By discharging internal matter outward at high velocity, a vehicle could hover, climb, descend, and accelerate in any medium or in a complete vacuum.

Fedorov outlined a reaction-propelled craft utilizing compressed gas mixtures. While his mechanical design contained several mechanical engineering shortcomings, publication of the monograph produced a strong impression upon the scientific community.

Most significantly: **this very monograph was read in Kaluga in 1896 by the young high-school mathematics teacher Constantine Tsiolkovskiy!** Tsiolkovskiy later recalled in several memoirs that Fedorov's work served as the direct catalyst prompting him to abandon conventional aeronautical concepts and dedicate his life to the **rigorous mathematical dynamics of rocket engines and the physics of cosmic flight**.
